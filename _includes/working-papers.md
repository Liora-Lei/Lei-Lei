### Demand Shocks and the Financing of Product Quality: Evidence from Hospitals

with *Jie Cao, Linjia Song, and Xintong Zhan*

<p class="paper-abstract" lang="en">Abstract: <em>We study the impact of demand shocks on product quality in the hospital sector. Exploiting staggered paid sick leave mandates as plausibly exogenous increases in healthcare demand, we document significant improvements in hospital quality across multiple dimensions. These effects are not explained by preventive care behavior, changes in patient severity, or local socioeconomic conditions. Instead, the evidence supports a financial channel: demand shocks improve hospitals’ financial conditions, lower external financing costs, and increase investment and employment. Quality improvements are more pronounced among financially constrained hospitals and hospitals with stronger governance that allocates these additional financial resources to quality-improving uses.</em></p>

### Public Firm Disclosure Spillovers and Retail Transaction Costs in Municipal Bond Markets Around IPOs

with *Jie Cao, Stephanie Cheng, and Walid Saffar*

<p class="paper-abstract" lang="en">Abstract: <em>We study whether public firm disclosure and its dissemination improve outcomes for retail investors in markets that do not produce timely disclosures themselves. Retail investors’ significant information-processing costs mean that more public disclosure need not reduce their informational disadvantage. We examine IPO-induced disclosure spillovers in the municipal bond secondary market, an opaque over-the-counter setting with weak issuer disclosure. Local IPOs generate mandatory disclosure and attract analyst and media coverage that can lower processing costs. Using restricted-access MSRB data, we find that round-trip dealer markups on retail-sized trades decline by 16 basis points after a local IPO, with muted institutional effects. Declines are larger when IPO-related disclosures are richer and issuer environments are poorer, smaller when processing frictions are higher, and larger when dealers' ex ante informational advantage is greater, but mitigated when dealer market power is stronger. Mandatory disclosure and its dissemination can thus compress dealer information rents and lower retail transaction costs beyond the disclosing firm.</em></p>

### The Valuation of Green Products

with *Jie Cao, Dongmei Li, Xintong Zhan, and Weiming Zhang*

<p class="paper-abstract" lang="en">Abstract: <em>We use trademark registrations to identify green product activity in firms’ core businesses and examine whether equity markets fully incorporate its value implications. Firms with such activity exhibit stronger subsequent operating performance and earn positive risk-adjusted returns, while analysts underestimate their future earnings. Green product activity predicts future stock returns after controlling for overall trademark intensity, green patent activity, standard return predictors, and industry effects. Abnormal returns are concentrated in the first post-formation year and in earnings-announcement months. Return predictability is stronger among firms with weaker environmental profiles, greater regulatory exposure, and greater valuation difficulty, as well as during periods of lower environmental salience. Overall, our findings are consistent with the gradual incorporation of value-relevant information about green product commercialization into stock prices, with stronger underreaction among firms with weaker environmental profiles and when the economic implications of green product activity are more difficult to assess.</em></p>
